Being the person who can fix everything is a tempting career plan.

You get the difficult tickets. People ask your opinion. A release gets stuck and you can make it move. There is immediate, visible evidence that you are useful.

There is also a way for this to become the team's permanent operating model. Every difficult change waits for the same person. Every architectural decision needs their interpretation. They take work back because finishing it is faster than explaining it. Nobody else gets enough experience in the awkward parts to become independent.

The person is busy. The team has a bottleneck.

I think seniority should make us more attentive to that distinction. The next useful contribution may be less satisfying than solving the problem personally. It may involve helping someone else form a plan, explaining which risk matters, and letting them finish the work differently from how we would have done it.

That last part is harder than the advice usually admits.

A team cannot develop judgment if every decision is graded against one engineer's private preferences. There needs to be room for a reasonable approach that isn't your favorite. Firm intervention should correspond to an actual constraint or risk. Preferences deserve to be identified as preferences.

This matters in review. Twenty cosmetic objections and one serious concern make the author do extra work finding the important part. A focused explanation of the concern can improve both the change and the person's understanding. The cosmetic points may simply teach them how to avoid another round of comments.

The same choice appears before implementation. Suppose a feature will let customers manage access. An experienced engineer can build the screen, or first notice that nobody has agreed who may grant which permissions and what revocation means for an existing session.

Resolving that ambiguity can prevent substantial rework. It is technical contribution even if the immediate output is a conversation and a better contract. The implementation would eventually have made those decisions anyway, possibly without anyone realizing they had been made.

A useful senior engineer makes that kind of risk visible while changing the plan is still cheap.

None of this is a reason to become detached from the code. There is an equally unhelpful version of seniority in which every difficulty becomes another diagram. Technical judgment needs contact with the system: the actual build, the failing test, the limitation in the API that the diagram politely omits.

A small spike can be the fastest way to test an assumption. Debugging a real failure can teach more about the architecture than another abstract debate. Writing code remains part of understanding what the team is asking other people to do.

The question is where personal implementation does the most good. Establishing a useful approach can help several engineers proceed. Keeping every routine decision dependent on you has a different effect, even if your individual output looks excellent.

I'd also put recurring friction on the senior engineer's agenda. A slow verification loop, unclear ownership of a shared module, or a release step known by only one person can cost the team more than the next difficult algorithm. These problems deserve attention before they cause a dramatic enough failure to get it.

Organizations have to recognize the contribution. If individual ticket throughput is the only work that counts, people will optimize for it. Asking for mentorship while treating it as time away from delivery produces predictable results.

Look for changes in what the team can do. Can more people modify a difficult area safely? Has review stopped accumulating behind one person? Does a recurring question now have an answer people can find? Those outcomes require judgment to evaluate, but so does almost everything worth doing in a complicated codebase.

During an incident, taking the keyboard may be exactly right. Afterward, the interesting work is making sure the next incident doesn't require finding the same person first.
